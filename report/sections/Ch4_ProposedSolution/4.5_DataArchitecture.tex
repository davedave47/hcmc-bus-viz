\section{Data Architecture}
\label{sec:solution:architecture}

The design problem addressed by the data architecture is the balance between
interactive query latency on the analytical surface and the build-time cost
of preparing the data behind that surface. Given the fixed analytical
catalogue produced in Section~\ref{sec:solution:themes} and the latency
target stated in requirement N1 of
Section~\ref{sec:solution:problem:nonfunctional}, the architecture must
guarantee bounded query cost for every chart in the catalogue while keeping
the warehouse small enough to satisfy the on-disk size limit of requirement
N4. This section evaluates three candidate architectures against these
constraints and selects one.

\subsection{Architecture Candidates}
\label{sec:solution:architecture:candidates}

Three architecture lineages dominate the analytical-warehouse
literature: the Medallion lakehouse pattern
\cite{databricksMedallion}, the Inmon Corporate Information Factory
\cite{inmon2008cif}, and the Data Vault 2.0 method
\cite{linstedt2015dv}. The Medallion pattern, popularised by the
Databricks Lakehouse, partitions a warehouse into three named tiers
(Bronze raw, Silver conformed, Gold aggregated) and is the lightest
of the three: a small set of tiers each with a single concern, and no
methodological apparatus beyond the tier definitions themselves. The
Inmon CIF assembles an enterprise-scale normalised data model around
a third-normal-form atomic warehouse with subject-area data marts
projecting from it; the model is designed for enterprise BI with many
consumers and a long history of conformed definitions, and the
methodological overhead (canonical naming, change-management,
slowly-changing-dimension typology) is paid by every project that
adopts it. The Data Vault 2.0 method introduces hub, link, and
satellite tables for full audit history and parallel ingestion of
many sources; the method shines when the warehouse must record every
change to every entity over time, with provenance back to the
source system that emitted each row.

The proposed system has a single application as its sole consumer, a
fixed 90-day analytical window, and no audit-history requirement;
the methodological overhead of Inmon CIF or Data Vault is therefore
not amortised against any of the project's stated goals. The
proposed system adopts the Medallion lineage and develops a specific
instantiation of it within Section~\ref{sec:solution:architecture:chosen}.
The three candidate architectures evaluated below are all instances
of, or variants on, the Medallion pattern.

The first candidate is the classical three-tier Medallion with the
Gold tier left as an on-demand transformation rather than as a
materialised table. The Bronze and Silver tiers are persistent, and
every chart query in the analytical surface runs a fresh aggregation
against the Silver tier at request time. The strength of this
candidate is the minimum materialisation cost: only the Silver tier
takes any meaningful disk space, and a schema change in the chart
catalogue requires no warehouse rebuild. The weakness in the present
context is that the on-demand aggregation step cannot meet the
warm-render latency target for an interactive analytical surface
that issues hundreds of small queries per user session, as the
measured Silver-only column of Table~\ref{tab:arch:quantitative}
shows.

A second candidate is a star-schema design placed directly over the raw
views produced by preprocessing, without a conformed dimensional layer. The
strength of this candidate is the minimal materialisation cost: only the
fact tables and the dimension tables would need to be created. The weakness
is that the route-identifier reconciliation between the GPS stream and the
AFC stream must be repeated in every analytical query, with the risk that
two visualisations referring to ``route 152'' refer to slightly different
subsets of vehicles or trips if the reconciliation logic drifts.

A third candidate, and the one selected, is a Medallion variant
\cite{databricksMedallion} in which the Silver layer is internally
dimensionalised and the Gold layer is materialised as pre-computed
aggregate tables. The architecture preserves the three named
Medallion tiers (Bronze, Silver, Gold) at the top level: Bronze
contains the raw views over the preprocessed data, Silver contains
the conformed dimensions and the fact views shaped through them, and
Gold contains the per-chart aggregate tables that the analytical
surface reads from. The internal organisation of the Silver layer
follows the Kimball dimensional pattern --- one dimension table per
conformed entity, plus narrow fact views that compose them --- so
that join-key uniqueness is enforced at one place rather than
re-derived in every chart query. The combined strength of the
candidate is therefore the recognition of the Medallion vocabulary at
the architectural level, the integrity guarantees of a dimensional
model at the Silver level, and the bounded query cost of a
pre-computed aggregate at the Gold level. The weakness is that the
build-time cost is higher than the other two alternatives, since the
Gold tables must be re-materialised whenever the underlying data
changes.

\subsection{Architecture Comparison}
\label{sec:solution:architecture:quantitative}

The qualitative comparison above is grounded by measurements taken
against the running Phase~One demo. Phase~One built the
warehouse end-to-end through all three Medallion tiers and the
benchmark script in \texttt{demo/warehouse/benchmark.py} measures, for
each of the seven Gold aggregates that back the chart catalogue, the
median wall-clock latency of two execution modes:

\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Silver only} --- the chart aggregate runs as a
      \texttt{GROUP BY} directly against the L3 fact view
      \texttt{fact\_boardings}, exactly as a plain Medallion build
      without a materialised Gold layer would serve it.
\item \emph{Silver + Gold} --- the chart aggregate reads from the
      pre-computed Gold table (\texttt{agg\_*}), which is what the
      running demo does.
\end{itemize}

Both modes share the same Bronze and Silver layers, so the difference
is exactly the value of the Gold tier. The two pass-or-fail gates
remain the warm re-render budget of fifty milliseconds set by
requirement N1 and the preprocessed on-disk size budget of one hundred
megabytes set by requirement N4. The measurement configuration is the
Phase-One workstation running DuckDB~1.1 in-process against the
\(17{,}883{,}623\)-row AFC stream and the locally available
single-day GPS extract; numbers are the median of five runs per query.

Five of the six Gold aggregates land in the single-digit-millisecond
band; the sixth (\texttt{agg\_stop\_hour}) is bounded by its row count
(\(1{,}339{,}100\) rows) rather than by the underlying query, and is
the only Gold table the demo does not yet render on the hottest chart
path. The total cost of serving the chart catalogue against the
Silver layer alone --- the configuration a plain Medallion build
would expose --- is approximately \(5.5\) seconds per page load,
which fails the warm re-render budget of requirement N1 by two orders
of magnitude on the daily-volume and hub-stats queries. With the Gold
tier materialised, the same catalogue is served in approximately
\(0.92\) seconds total per page load, and every aggregate except
\texttt{agg\_stop\_hour} sits well inside the fifty-millisecond
ceiling. The headline ratio is a sixfold reduction in cumulative
query cost, achieved by adding approximately \(30\) megabytes of
materialised Gold tables to the warehouse file --- a one-time
build-step expense that is paid offline at data-refresh boundaries
and recovered within the first interactive page load.

Three observations frame the selection. The storage budget of
requirement N4 is satisfied by all three candidates at the warehouse
level because the columnar engine's compression brings the AFC stream
to approximately seventy megabytes regardless of whether the Gold layer
is materialised; the materialised Gold tables add approximately thirty
megabytes more, leaving the warehouse file below the
one-hundred-megabyte target. The Phase-One development workspace
retains raw CSV sources and full daily parquet partitions alongside the
warehouse file and therefore currently exceeds the N4 bound; the target
in Phase Two is a Gold-only deployment that discards the raw staging
layer. Storage is therefore not a discriminator between the three
architectural candidates.
The warm re-render budget of requirement N1, on the other hand, is
satisfied only by the selected Medallion-with-Gold variant: on-demand
aggregation over the \(17{,}883{,}623\)-row AFC stream cannot complete
within fifty milliseconds for any of the six aggregates measured in
Table~\ref{tab:arch:quantitative}, even after column pruning and
predicate pushdown. The build-time penalty of materialising the Gold
layer --- a single pass that completes in approximately five seconds
on the local workstation --- is paid offline at data-refresh
boundaries and is amortised across every interactive session.

The latency numbers in Table~\ref{tab:arch:quantitative} are
measured, not projected. They were captured by the same warehouse
that serves the running demo, against the same DuckDB engine, with
both modes warmed in the columnar cache before timing. The selected
Medallion-with-Gold variant's latency advantage is therefore
empirical: the cumulative cost of serving the chart catalogue drops
from approximately five-and-a-half seconds per page load (Silver
only) to approximately nine-hundred milliseconds (Silver + Gold),
with the hottest two paths --- the hub stats strip and the
daily-volume timeline --- collapsing from hundreds of milliseconds to
sub-millisecond.

Figure~\ref{fig:arch:tradeoff} places the three candidates on the
(build cost, warm p99 latency) plane defined by the two pass gates of

the dominance argument visually direct: only the Medallion-with-Gold candidate
sits inside the latency budget of requirement N1, while all three
candidates respect the on-disk budget of requirement N4. The
build-cost penalty of the Medallion-with-Gold design is paid offline at
data-refresh boundaries; the warm-latency advantage is paid forward at
every interactive chart query.

Two subsidiary benchmarks validate supporting claims. An in-process
LRU cache applied to the Silver-only configuration matches Gold
warm-read latency but incurs the full Silver cost on cold start and

\subsection{Selected Architecture}
\label{sec:solution:architecture:chosen}

The proposed system adopts the Medallion architecture with a
materialised Gold layer and a dimensionally-organised Silver layer.
The selection is forced by the two pass gates of
Table~\ref{tab:arch:quantitative}: only the Medallion-with-Gold
variant satisfies the warm re-render budget of requirement N1 while
also satisfying the on-disk size budget of requirement N4. The
higher build-time cost is acceptable because the build is run rarely
(once per data refresh) and because the preprocessed window is
bounded (90 days of AFC and 50 days of GPS at the upper limit).

\subsection{Refresh Policy}
\label{sec:solution:architecture:refresh}

The Silver dimension tables and the Gold aggregate tables are
persistent on the on-disk warehouse file. They are re-created in a
single deterministic pass when the upstream preprocessed data
changes; the pass is the warehouse build referenced in requirement
N3 and is implemented in \texttt{demo/warehouse/build.py}. The Silver
fact views are not materialised, because the columnar storage of the
Bronze layer already supports the scan throughput required by the
Gold build and DuckDB pushes predicates and projections through the
view boundary; materialising the facts would duplicate seventeen
million rows on disk for no measurable behaviour change. The Gold
build therefore reads from Bronze through the lightweight Silver fact
views and writes once to the persistent Gold tables. The total
on-disk size of the warehouse file remains below the limit set in
requirement N4 because the Gold tier compresses the AFC stream into a
few hundred thousand rows across seven aggregate tables.

\subsection{Consistency}
\label{sec:solution:architecture:guarantees}

The warehouse build is idempotent: re-running the build on the same raw
input produces byte-identical conformed-dimension tables and
byte-identical aggregate tables, which satisfies requirement N3. The build
ordering respects the layer dependency: conformed dimensions are rebuilt
before any aggregate that depends on them, and aggregates are written only
after every dimension on which they depend has been written successfully.
At startup, the analytical surface validates the presence of all seven
aggregate tables and refuses to render any theme whose backing aggregate
is missing or whose modification timestamp is older than the most recent
upstream change; the missing or stale theme is reported to the user with a
short diagnostic note rather than rendered against incomplete data.
