\section{Analytical Themes}
\label{sec:solution:themes}

The proposed system organises its analytical content into six thematic
views. Each theme corresponds to one class of analytical question and is
supported by one cluster of aggregate tables produced by layer 4 of the data
architecture (Section~\ref{sec:solution:architecture}). The themes are the
top-level organisational units of the analytical surface; the user
navigates between themes from an overview index, and within each theme
selects a single visualisation that addresses the question of interest.

\subsection{Theme Structure}
\label{sec:solution:themes:six}

Table~\ref{tab:themes:six} lists the six themes with the analytical
question each one answers, the visualisation primitive that anchors the
theme, and the cross-theme dependencies that link it to the rest of the
catalogue. The primary user role column is an internal design grounding;
the user interface exposes no role labels.

The 14 stakeholder questions cluster into 6 themes under two
criteria: questions in the same theme share both their primary backing
aggregate table (so a single warehouse query serves multiple charts in
the theme) and their dominant analytical primitive (so the
\emph{one-primitive-per-theme} invariant of
Section~\ref{sec:solution:interaction:storytelling} is satisfiable
without re-introducing duplicated lenses). Questions that satisfy
neither criterion remain separate themes; the resulting 6-theme
catalogue is one of several defensible clusterings.

The School-Aware Demand theme is the most distinctive component of the
proposed system; it is the theme that no publicly accessible tool for
Ho Chi Minh City currently provides, and it is the theme that connects most
directly to the dominant fare class observed in
Section~\ref{sec:solution:dataset:eda}. The distinction is operational
rather than methodological: the visualisation primitives that the theme
uses (bubble constellation, treemap, calendar heatmap, point map) are
standard, but no existing tool has applied them to the AFC student-fare
share in this network. Two evidential boundaries that bear on the
theme's strength are stated up front rather than buried inside
Section~\ref{sec:solution:viz:school}. \textit{First}, the headline
finding that student fares carry approximately 36~percent of network
boardings is a property of the AFC fare-class label and is supported
directly by the data. \textit{Second}, the per-school attribution that
the theme's visualisations rely on is hand-verified for the eight largest
universities only; the remaining schools are geocoded at district
granularity and student tickets whose boarding stop falls outside
walking distance of any geocoded school are reported under an
\textit{unattributed} bucket. Per-school claims in this theme therefore
operate at full confidence on the eight-university subset and at reduced
confidence elsewhere; the interface surfaces a verified-or-stubbed flag
on every school view so that the boundary is visible to the reader at
all times. The remaining five themes cover network-wide demand, spatial
coverage, financial operations, reliability and anomalies, and per-route
detail respectively, so that every functional requirement in
Section~\ref{sec:solution:problem:functional} is addressed by at least
one theme.

\subsection{Theme Alternatives}
\label{sec:solution:themes:alt}

Two alternative organisations of the analytical content were considered and
rejected.

The first alternative groups content by user role rather than by analytical
question: one theme each for passenger, operating company, and public-transport
authority. The alternative was rejected because the most
important analytical questions cross role boundaries. The school-aware
demand question, for example, is asked by both passengers (which route
serves my school?) and by the authority (which corridors carry the largest
student loads?); a role-keyed organisation would either duplicate the
content across two themes or assign it arbitrarily to one of them, with the
other role losing access to the question. A question-keyed organisation
sidesteps the duplication by anchoring each theme to a single question that
all roles can ask.

The second alternative groups content by visualisation primitive: one
theme for maps, one for time series, one for ranked bar charts, and so on.
The alternative was rejected because end users do not formulate questions
about chart types. A primitive-keyed catalogue would force the user to
recognise the chart type that answers their question before they could
locate the relevant theme, which inverts the natural exploration order. A
question-keyed catalogue lets the user start from the question and reach
the chart afterwards.

In summary, the three organisations considered are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{One theme per user role} (rejected): cross-role questions would be duplicated or arbitrarily assigned.
\item \textbf{One theme per visualisation primitive} (rejected): inverts the natural exploration order, since the user would need to recognise the chart type before reaching the question.
\item \textbf{Six themes, one per analytical question} (selected): each theme starts from a question end users naturally ask.
\end{itemize}

The chosen six-theme catalogue elevates the analytical question to the
top-level organisational unit and treats the supporting visualisations as
subordinate to the question. The per-theme visualisation selection that
follows in Section~\ref{sec:solution:viz} respects this subordination by
selecting each visualisation to fit the question rather than the reverse.

\subsection{Prioritisation}
\label{sec:solution:themes:prioritisation}

Not all six themes carry equal weight in the phase 1 implementation. The
themes are ordered (without changing the themes themselves) along two
axes: how dependent each theme is on operational data that is only
partially available locally, and how much novelty each theme contributes
relative to the existing tools surveyed in
Chapter~\ref{chapter:related}. The school-aware theme is the most
distinctive deliverable of Phase~2 because no surveyed tool addresses
the educational-institution dimension of demand; the Phase~2 roadmap
(Section~\ref{sec:future:roadmap}) accordingly sequences it after the
Demand and Crowding theme (which establishes the warehouse-to-surface
plumbing) and before the Spatial, Financial, and Network-Health themes. The demand-and-crowding,
spatial-and-connectivity, and financial-and-operations themes follow;
they also depend primarily on the AFC stream and on the static master
tables, both of which are complete. The network-health theme and the
per-route reliability indicators reach full coverage only after the full
50-day GPS window is loaded and the schedule rows have been cleaned,
both of which are recorded as Phase~2 prerequisites in
Chapter~\ref{chapter:future}.

\subsection{Stories}
\label{sec:solution:themes:stories}

Four of the six themes are paired with a Story: a curated, scroll-locked
sequence of chart states through that theme's canvases that walks the
reader from a single dramatic question to a recap and call to action. The
themes paired with stories are School-Aware Demand (the school commute, in
numbers), Spatial and Connectivity (where the city actually rides),
Financial and Operations (who runs Saigon's buses), and Network Health and
Performance (nine days without a network, on the Tết shock). The remaining
two themes, Demand and Crowding and Route Detail, are tool-mode only in the
present phase.

Stories are not a separate analytical theme and are not a separate
top-level destination in the user interface. They are a second reading
mode within each of the four source themes, available alongside the
free-exploration mode through an inline affordance on the theme's entry
in the overview index. The same chart components and the same warehouse aggregates serve
both reading modes; the Story mode adds only a sequence specification
(which charts to show in which order with which filters and annotations)
plus an opening hero and closing recap that follow the three-act spine
described in Section~\ref{sec:foundations:storytelling}. Holding Stories
inside the themes keeps the navigation surface compact (six themes, rather
than six themes plus a separate Stories destination) and ensures that any
chart state encountered inside a Story can be peeled back into free
exploration without leaving the theme.

The Story mode is implemented as sticky-graphic scrollytelling rather
than as a click-through carousel, following the pattern of the
VnExpress 2025 labour-market data-journalism
piece~\cite{vnexpress2025labour}. A Story is divided into scroll
sections; within a section the chart is pinned to the viewport with
sticky positioning so that it stays in place while narrative
step panels scroll past it. As each step panel crosses the trigger line,
the pinned chart changes \emph{state} in response: a highlight overlay
(one element brought to focal accent while the rest fade), an
annotation overlay (a label or reference line appears with a
connecting prose sentence), a filter change (the chart re-renders
against a different subset, for example a different fare class), or an
encode change (a visual channel such as bubble colour switches to a
different metric). A change of chart type, when a Story needs one,
happens at a section boundary rather than mid-scroll: each section
pins one chart, and the reader moves to the next pinned chart by
scrolling into the next section, so the chart never morphs under the
reader within a section. The convention keeps the reader's attention
on the pinned chart while the narrative unfolds, rather than splitting
attention between paragraph and figure as a conventional report does.

Story scenes draw on the two-tier chart sourcing of
Table~\ref{tab:themes:chart_categories}. Most scroll-section scenes
pin a tool-mode catalogue chart in a frozen story state: a route-by-hour
heatmap with a single column highlighted, a constellation with one
quadrant brought forward, a network pulse with the Tết window shaded.
These are derived states of an existing canvas and require no new
chart module. A small number of scenes pin a bespoke simple story
chart that exists only inside a Story (the free-fare lift slope and
the resilience sparkline grid), which is the second sourcing tier; such
a chart is deliberately simpler than any tool-catalogue chart because a
narrative beat needs none of the interaction the tool catalogue
requires.

\subsubsection*{Two-Tier Chart Sourcing}

Story charts are drawn from two tiers
(Table~\ref{tab:themes:chart_categories}). The first tier reuses a
tool-mode catalogue chart in a frozen \emph{story state} (a particular
filter, highlight, or annotation). The second tier introduces a
\emph{bespoke simple story chart} that exists only inside a Story and
never in the tool catalogue. The second tier is the design's
deliberate departure from the earlier assumption that Story mode
consumes only the tool catalogue: a narrative beat needs none of the
Select-Filter-Abstract interaction that the tool catalogue requires of
every chart, so a dead-simple narrative chart (a unit-dot array, a
single annotated line, a before-and-after slope, a small sparkline
grid) is the correct tool for a Story beat even though it would fail
the tool-catalogue inclusion criteria. The pattern follows the
VnExpress data-journalism piece, whose story charts are bespoke per
narrative beat rather than reused from an analytical dashboard.
